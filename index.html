    Binance's private keys never leaked. The attacker didn't need them.

On Oct 29, about $6.3B left Binance hot wallets across 10-11 chains, including 18,270 $BTC. Binance's first update points to a compromised DevOps engineer's laptop that reached a service able to submit withdrawals. TRM, Elliptic and Blockaid describe the same thing: the signing system itself approved the transfers.

That's the uncomfortable part. "Secure keys" means little if the software that uses them can be told what to sign.

Takeaway for spot holders: custody isn't only where the key lives. It's who, or what, can make it sign.

If you self-custody, what actually approves your transactions: you, or a machine you trust?

    How a chain liquidation works, step by step:

1. A headline hits (today: the Binance hack) and price drops
2. The most leveraged longs lose their margin
3. The exchange closes them with market sells, at any price
4. Those sells push price into the next layer of liquidation levels
5. Repeat until the leverage is flushed out

Nobody decides to sell in steps 3–5. The engine does it.

Spot Bitcoin with no debt has no liquidation price. Nobody can sell it for you.

The drop that wipes out your margin ≈ 100% ÷ leverage.

Fees and maintenance margin make it even closer.
